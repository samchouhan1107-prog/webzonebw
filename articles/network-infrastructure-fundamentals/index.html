Enterprise Network Infrastructure & Subnetting: The Practical IT Guide

One company • Five subnets • A network built step by step

📅 Published: September 20, 2026 · ⏱️ Read Time: 12 min
Networking & Infrastructure

1. Meet TechCorp: A Network in the Making

Imagine TechCorp, a growing technology company with 250 employees spread across three floors. The sales team works from the ground floor, engineering occupies the second, and management sits on the third. There are 50 company laptops, 20 desktop workstations, 10 IP phones, 5 network printers, 8 servers in the data center, and a guest Wi-Fi network that visitors use daily.

Every single one of these devices needs connectivity. But here's the challenge: a sales representative's laptop shouldn't be able to directly access the finance database, visitors shouldn't reach internal systems, and a broadcast storm from one department shouldn't crash the entire company.

This is where enterprise network infrastructure begins. We're not just connecting devices—we're building a structured system where every device has its place, every connection has purpose, and security is built into the architecture from the ground up.

Let's build this network the way an IT professional would: starting with the people and devices, figuring out how they need to communicate, dividing the network where necessary, connecting those networks safely, and preparing for when something inevitably goes wrong.

// Our target network architecture
Employee Devices → 192.168.10.0/24
Servers → 192.168.20.0/24
Voice Network → 192.168.30.0/24
Management → 192.168.40.0/24
Guest Network → 192.168.50.0/24

By the end of this guide, every line in that architecture diagram will represent a concrete, configured component—not just a theoretical concept.

2. Every Device Needs an Identity: IPv4 Addressing

Before any device can join our network, it needs a unique address. In the IPv4 world, that address is a 32-bit number typically written as four decimal numbers separated by dots (like 192.168.1.10). But the real question is: how do we determine which part of this address identifies the network itself, and which part identifies the specific device (host) on that network?

The answer lies in the subnet mask and CIDR notation. When we write /24, we're saying "the first 24 bits of this address identify the network, and the remaining 8 bits identify the host." This is the foundation of all modern IP networking.

The relationship is straightforward: higher prefix numbers mean fewer host bits, which means smaller subnets. Here's how to calculate subnet sizes on the fly:

Subnet Calculation Formula:

For a prefix length /n:

  • Host bits: 32 - n
  • Total addresses: 2^(32-n)
  • Usable hosts: 2^(32-n) - 2 (network and broadcast addresses can't be used)

Those two reserved addresses are why a "254-host" subnet can't actually host 256 devices—a detail that catches many people off guard when planning networks.

Instead of memorizing every possible subnet size, engineers typically remember the common ones and calculate anything else as needed:

CIDR Prefix Subnet Mask Total Addresses Usable Hosts Common Use Case
/30 255.255.255.252 4 2 Point-to-point links between routers
/28 255.255.255.240 16 14 Small networks, management interfaces
/24 255.255.255.0 256 254 Standard department networks
/22 255.255.252.0 1,024 1,022 Large office spaces, campus Wi-Fi

Checkpoint: Why does a /30 subnet only provide two usable addresses instead of four?

Because the first address is reserved as the network identifier and the last address is reserved for broadcast traffic. This leaves exactly two addresses for the two endpoints of a point-to-point connection, which is precisely what a /30 is designed for.

3. One Big Network Creates Big Problems

Imagine putting all 250+ devices from TechCorp onto a single /22 network (1,022 usable addresses). Technically, it would work. But practically, it would be a disaster waiting to happen.

Here's why single large networks fail:

  • Broadcast storms: A misconfigured device can send broadcast packets that flood every device on the network
  • Security risks: A compromised laptop in sales could potentially reach sensitive finance or HR systems
  • Performance issues: ARP requests and other broadcast traffic consume bandwidth
  • Troubleshooting nightmare: When something goes wrong, it's impossible to isolate the problem

The solution is network segmentation—dividing the large network into smaller, more manageable pieces. Each segment becomes its own broadcast domain, with its own security boundaries and performance characteristics.

This is where subnetting comes in. We break our address space into smaller chunks, each serving a specific purpose. But subnetting alone isn't enough—we also need VLANs to enforce those boundaries at the layer 2 level.

Checkpoint: Two devices are in different IP networks. Can they communicate directly without a router?

No. Devices in different IP networks are in different broadcast domains. They require a router (or layer 3 switch) to forward traffic between them. This router becomes the natural enforcement point for security policies between segments.

4. Planning Subnets for Real Device Counts

Let's design our subnets based on actual requirements. We'll use a private address space (192.168.0.0/16) and allocate specific subnets for each department and function.

Here's our subnet planning process:

# Employee Network Planning
Requirement: 70 employee devices
Host calculation: Need at least 70 usable addresses
Prefix selection: /26 (64 usable addresses - perfect fit)
Network address: 192.168.10.0/26
First usable: 192.168.10.1
Last usable: 192.168.10.62
Broadcast: 192.168.10.63

Let's plan all our subnets systematically:

Department Device Count Subnet CIDR Usable Range
Employee Devices 70 192.168.10.0/26 /26 192.168.10.1 - 192.168.10.62
Servers 8 192.168.20.0/28 /28 192.168.20.1 - 192.168.20.14
Voice Network 10 192.168.30.0/28 /28 192.168.30.1 - 192.168.30.14
Management 5 192.168.40.0/29 /29 192.168.40.1 - 192.168.40.6
Guest Network 20 192.168.50.0/27 /27 192.168.50.1 - 192.168.50.30

Notice how we've sized each subnet appropriately for its actual device count, rather than using arbitrary sizes. The employee network uses a /26 because we need space for 70 devices, while the management network uses a /29 since we only need addresses for 5 devices.

5. Creating Logical Boundaries with VLANs

Our IP addressing plan draws lines on paper, but switches don't enforce those boundaries automatically. That's the job of VLANs (Virtual Local Area Networks). Each VLAN creates a separate broadcast domain, ensuring that broadcast traffic from one VLAN never reaches devices in another VLAN.

In practice, we map one VLAN to one subnet from our plan above. This alignment makes the network much easier to understand and troubleshoot:

VLAN ID Department Subnet Purpose
10 Employee 192.168.10.0/26 Staff laptops and desktops
20 Servers 192.168.20.0/28 Database and application servers
30 Voice 192.168.30.0/28 IP phones and VoIP systems
40 Management 192.168.40.0/29 Switch and server management interfaces
50 Guest 192.168.50.0/27 Visitor devices with internet-only access

Checkpoint: A laptop in VLAN 10 needs to reach a server in VLAN 20. Both are connected to the same physical switch. Can the communication happen directly?

No. Different VLANs are separate broadcast domains and separate IP networks. Traffic between them must be routed through a router or layer 3 switch. This routing process is where we can apply security policies and control what traffic flows between segments.

6. Connecting the Segments: Gateways and Routing

Once we have our VLANs and subnets defined, we need a way for traffic to flow between them. This is where routers (or layer 3 switches) come into play as the default gateways for each subnet.

Default Gateway Function

Every device on a subnet needs a default gateway—the IP address of the router that handles traffic destined for other networks. For our employee network (192.168.10.0/26), the default gateway would typically be 192.168.10.1.

When a device wants to send traffic to another subnet, it sends the packet to its default gateway. The router then examines the destination IP address and forwards it to the appropriate next hop.

Inter-VLAN Routing

On a layer 3 switch, we create a "switched virtual interface" (SVI) for each VLAN. Each SVI gets an IP address from its corresponding subnet and acts as the default gateway for that VLAN.

# Layer 3 Switch Configuration Example
interface Vlan10
  ip address 192.168.10.1 255.255.255.192
  description Employee Network Gateway

interface Vlan20
  ip address 192.168.20.1 255.255.255.240
  description Server Network Gateway

interface Vlan30
  ip address 192.168.30.1 255.255.255.240
  description Voice Network Gateway

Static vs Dynamic Routing

For small networks like TechCorp, static routes work well. We configure the router with specific paths to each subnet. For larger networks, dynamic routing protocols (like OSPF or EIGRP) automatically discover and maintain network paths.

The default route on our edge router points to the internet connection, ensuring that any traffic destined for outside our network gets routed properly.

7. Making the Network Usable: DHCP and DNS

Even with perfect addressing and routing, our network wouldn't be practical without two essential services: DHCP (Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol) and DNS (Domain Name System).

DHCP: Automatic Address Assignment

DHCP eliminates the need to manually configure IP addresses on every device. It operates in four stages:

  1. Discover: A new device broadcasts to find DHCP servers
  2. Offer: DHCP servers respond with available addresses and configuration
  3. Request: The client selects one offer and formally requests it
  4. Acknowledge: The server confirms the assignment and sets a lease time

One challenge: routers don't forward DHCP broadcasts. To solve this, we configure "IP helper addresses" on our VLAN interfaces to convert broadcasts to unicasts directed at our DHCP server.

DNS: Name Resolution

Users remember names like files.techcorp.local or webzonebw.in, not IP addresses. DNS translates these names into the IP addresses that computers actually use.

When DNS fails, the symptoms are misleading: browsers "stop working" even though the network itself is fine. A disproportionate number of "internet is down" issues turn out to be DNS resolution problems.

That's why our troubleshooting routine always includes testing both IP connectivity (using ping 1.1.1.1) and name resolution (using nslookup webzonebw.in) separately.

Checkpoint: A user reports they can't access internal servers. What's the first thing you should check about their network configuration?

Start with their IP address, subnet mask, and default gateway. If these are correct, then check DNS resolution. Many connectivity issues boil down to incorrect network settings or DNS problems.

8. The Network Needs Rules: Security Boundaries

Routing creates the roads between our network segments, but firewalls decide who's allowed to drive on them. Instead of treating security as an afterthought, we build it into our network architecture from the beginning.

Here's our security policy for TechCorp:

From To Policy
Employee (VLAN 10) Servers (VLAN 20) Allow specific application ports only (SMB, RDP, database connections)
Guest (VLAN 50) Internal Networks Deny entirely; internet access only
Employee (VLAN 10) Management (VLAN 40) Deny by default; only IT admin workstations allowed
Voice (VLAN 30) Servers (VLAN 20) Allow to PBX and call-control server only; QoS tagged

A key principle: every "allow" rule must be justified, and everything else should be denied. Networks configured this way fail loudly and predictably, making them much easier to troubleshoot than networks held together by undocumented exceptions.

9. When Something Goes Wrong: Systematic Troubleshooting

Every network fails eventually. What separates manageable outages from chaotic ones is having a systematic troubleshooting routine that narrows down the problem layer by layer.

Here's our troubleshooting sequence, starting from the device and working outward:

# Step 1: Verify local TCP/IP stack
ping 127.0.0.1

# Step 2: Check local network connectivity
ping 192.168.10.1

# Step 3: Test internet connectivity (bypasses DNS)
ping 1.1.1.1

# Step 4: Test name resolution
nslookup webzonebw.in

# Step 5: Trace the network path
tracert 1.1.1.1

Each step only matters if the previous one passed:

  • Step 1 fails: Network stack issue—reinstall or reset TCP/IP
  • Step 2 fails: Local network issue—cable, switch port, or VLAN assignment
  • Step 3 fails but Step 2 passes: Routing or firewall issue between local network and internet
  • Step 4 fails but Step 3 passes: DNS problem, not a network issue
  • Step 5 shows specific hop: Problem at that specific network device

Checkpoint: A user reports "no internet." ping 192.168.10.1 works, but ping 1.1.1.1 times out. Where should you investigate first?

Start with the default route configuration on the local gateway, then check the edge firewall rules. The local network segment is fine since the gateway responds, so the problem lies in the path to the internet.

10. Putting It All Together: The Complete Network

Let's review what we've built. Starting from the users and working through the technology, we've created a network that's:

  • Organized: Each department and function has its own network segment
  • Secure: VLANs and firewalls prevent unauthorized access between segments
  • Scalable: Subnets are sized appropriately for actual device counts
  • Manageable: DHCP and DNS automate address assignment and name resolution
  • Troubleshootable: Clear segmentation makes problems easier to isolate

The same progression we followed—users first, then addressing, then segmentation, then routing, then security—mirrors how real enterprise networks are designed. This approach ensures that each component serves a clear purpose and that the network grows with the organization rather than becoming a tangled mess of exceptions.

When you inherit a network or design a new one, start with the same questions: Who are the users? What devices do they need? How should those devices communicate? Where do we need boundaries? How do we connect those boundaries safely? How do we maintain and troubleshoot the result?

This systematic approach transforms networking from a collection of configuration commands into a structured engineering discipline—one that serves the organization's needs while maintaining security, performance, and manageability.
